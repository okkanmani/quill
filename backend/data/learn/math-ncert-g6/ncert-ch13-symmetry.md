A figure has **symmetry** when one half matches the other in a balanced way. Many designs in nature, art, and architecture use symmetry.

## Line symmetry (reflection symmetry)

If you can **fold** a figure along a line so the two halves **coincide exactly**, the figure has **line symmetry**.

That fold line is the **line of symmetry** (or **axis of symmetry**).

- A mirror placed on the line shows one half as the reflection of the other
- Some figures have **one** line of symmetry; others have **several** (e.g. a square has 4)

## Figures with and without symmetry

- **Symmetric:** butterfly, letter A (vertical line), equilateral triangle (3 lines)
- **Not symmetric:** many random scribbles — no fold line makes both halves match

## Making symmetric figures

**Ink-blot method:** fold paper, drop ink on one half, press — the print is symmetric about the fold.

**Paper cutting:** fold and cut shapes — unfold to see symmetric patterns.

## Symmetry in regular polygons

| Shape | Lines of symmetry |
|-------|-------------------|
| Equilateral triangle | 3 |
| Square | 4 |
| Regular pentagon | 5 |
| Regular hexagon | 6 |

A **regular** polygon with **n** sides often has **n** lines of symmetry.

## Reflection

Each point on one side of the line of symmetry has a matching point the **same distance** on the other side, directly across the line.

## Symmetry in daily life

Look for symmetry in:
- Floor tiles and rangoli patterns
- Faces (approximate left–right symmetry)
- Letters and logos
- Buildings (Taj Mahal, temples)

## Rotational symmetry (intro)

Some figures look the same after a **turn** about a point — that idea is developed further in later classes. At this stage, focus on **line symmetry** and the mirror/fold test.
