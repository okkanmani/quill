## Why graphs?

**Graphs (charts)** bundle numbers into visuals so brains spot **patterns**, **comparisons**, and **changes** quicker than sprawling tables alone. Analysts pair drawings with precise tables when accuracy matters—you read both deliberately.

Ontario classrooms often practise **creating**, **reflecting**, and **critiquing** representations in the **data literacy & probability** strands.

---

## Tables first (raw material)

Collect data with clear **variables** (“what changed?”):

| Example table | Columns |
|---|---|
| Classroom lunch orders | **Category** × **Counts** × maybe **Totals** |

Add a **grand total row** whenever you rewrite counts as fractions of the whole—you need the denominator.

**Important moves**

- Decide **WHO** counts (people, squirrels, litres of rain).
- Check **HOW** counted (survey class only? whole school?).
- Name **WHERE** plus **WHEN captured** (“April lab block” vs “lifetime average”).

Charts inherit those limits—charts **cannot magically fix sloppy scope**.

---

## Bar graphs (often categorical)

Bars compare **distinct categories**:

- Bars may run **horizontal**—choose whichever fits readability.
- **Consistent widths** anchor fair visual comparison.
- **Grid lines** alongside axis tick marks help interpolate between printed numbers.

**Stacked versus clustered**

- **Stacked bars** cram parts into wholes—great when relative contributions matter BUT hide absolute differences among bases.
- **Double (clustered) bars** juxtapose datasets (Grade 7 vs Grade 12 plant heights) cleanly when readers must eyeball contrasts.

Practice narrating comparisons in **additive** sentences (“Arts club grew by 42 members while Debate shrunk by nine.”).

---

## Line graphs — change over sequential order

Plots connect ordered measurements—typically **time** on the horizontal axis—to reveal **growth**, **decay**, or **cyclical bursts**.

Interpret features:

| Feature | Everyday question |
|---------|-------------------|
| **Steep uphill** | Faster increase per hop along the timeline |
| **Flat segment** | Stasis or maintenance |
| **Spike downward** | Drop / intervention / glitch—context decides |
| **Missing points** | Was data skipped or truly absent? annotate |

Forecasting future points **beyond** your data guesses—call guesses **predictions**, keep uncertainty plainly spoken.

---

## Pictographs (symbol charts)

Pictures replace bars with **symbols** keyed to quantity:

- Read the legend first (“⚽ = six students”).
- Partial icons often mean halves or quarters—explicitly clarified in good diagrams.
- Pictographs feel friendly but sloppy icons trick eyes—professional science reports favour measured bars/scatterplots.

Ask: “Would rectangles or precise numbers reduce misread risk?” Sometimes **dual encoding** icons + numeric labels solves both vibes and accuracy.

---

## Dot plots / number lines (light intro)

When each measurement repeats, stack **dots** above a calibrated line—cluster tightness screams **modes** and **clusters** without binning prematurely.

Dots hint at **variation**—“some classes finished labs in eighteen minutes while others stalled near thirty-seven.”

Stem-and-leaf plots share spirit but textual—save heavy detail for specialised lessons.

---

## Histograms versus bar charts

Bars between **distinct categories**, histogram **bins** summarise **ranges** along a continuum (time to finish test, beetle lengths). Histogram bar **touch** because neighbouring bins imply continuity assumptions.

Unequal bin widths distort area tricks—ethical analysts label frequencies clearly OR normalise densities.

Grade 5 may meet simplified histogram previews; treat them as grouped counts with explicit bin limits.

---

## Centre & spread intuition (before formal stats courses)

Beyond **mean (average)**

- **Median** survives **outliers** better for skewed allowances (millionaire crashing “average allowance” chat).
- **Mode** crowns the most repeated category—but mode may multiply (bimodal playlists) or vanish distinctly.

Discuss **spread** conversationally—“scores bunch near 74 but one hero at 109.” Later courses formalise quartiles/variance—you’re priming intuition now.

Mean reminder: sum ÷ count, always label units (**mean mass 12.8 kg**) and watch duplicate entries.

---

## Misleading visuals (ethical radar)

Critique prompts sharpen scientific literacy:

- **Truncated axes** exaggerate deltas when zero baseline vanished.
- **3D gimmicks & tilt** distort comparative areas.
- **Cherry picking windows** hides earlier downward trends (“show only rebound quarter”).
- **Dual axes** manipulating scales can falsely imply correlation (“ice cream vs shark attacks”).
- **Non-zero baselines without annotation** confuse casual readers—you must yell “axis starts at 320” visibly.

Responsible designers label breaks, annotate sources, and release underlying tables alongside shiny graphics.

---

## Correlation cautiously

Seeing two lines rise together sparks curiosity—yet **association ≠ causation**. Hidden variables (“community wealth funds both playgrounds and librarians”) sabotage causal claims drawn from naive charts.

Scientists demand **experiment design**, **controls**, cautious language **“consistent with…”** versus **“proves…”**.

---

## Communicating responsibly

Audience decides detail depth:

| Audience tweak | Goal |
|---|---|
| Public poster | Minimal jargon; highlight uncertainty |
| Classroom lab | Transparent units + raw table appendix |
| Community decision | Transparent sources + limitations paragraph |

Practice explaining **limitations** verbally—science fairs love “what we didn’t measure yet.”

---

## Mini workflow (analysis loop)

1. **Pose questions** BEFORE collecting (prevents drifting scope).
2. **Collect cleanly** noting limitations.
3. **Organise**: tables sort → charts emphasise storyline.
4. **Describe** distributions (shape, outliers, plausible stories).
5. **Infer cautiously**, propose next measurements.

Looping refinement mirrors professional data workflows—iterate instead of stamping “final Truth.”
